Where the agents intervene

DFT-GEN is not a one-step simplification prompt. The workflow separates extraction, diagnosis, controlled rewriting, verification, and reader-facing presentation.

1

Content preparation

  • Extract readable exhibition text.
  • Protect dates, dimensions, object names, proper nouns, and source-sensitive formatting.
  • Separate curatorial text from metadata such as accession numbers.
2

Architect Agent

  • Diagnoses likely dyslexia-related reading barriers.
  • Flags dense sentence chains, visual crowding, jargon, and weak reading sequence.
  • Marks terms that should be preserved or scaffolded.
3

Writer Agent

  • Rewrites into labelled micro-units.
  • Uses clear sequence: what it is, what it looks like, why it matters.
  • Applies the dyslexia-aware lexicon before producing the final wording.
4

Evaluator Agent

  • Compares the rewritten text against protected facts.
  • Flags omissions, unsupported claims, and meaning drift.
  • Triggers revision when fidelity or accessibility checks fail.
5

Reader interface

  • Restores protected formatting.
  • Displays standard, card, or Focus Mode views.
  • Provides spacing, highlighting, and line-by-line focus support.

Worked text-processing example

The example below uses shortened excerpts to show the transformation logic. It illustrates the same principle used for the 228 Palace Museum exhibition texts: preserve interpretive meaning while reducing processing burden.

Original source problem

The official exhibition text often compresses object identity, historical period, dimensions, visual description, and cultural meaning into a single dense curatorial passage.

For example, one passage may introduce an inkstone, report its measurements, describe the Lanting gathering motif, and explain why the imagery matters, all before giving the reader a clear path through the object.

  • High factual value.
  • High visual and sequential burden.
  • Key facts must not be removed.

One-step Helper risk

This is an inkstone.
It has a picture on it.
The picture shows people gathering.
It is easier to read, but many details are gone.
  • Surface accessibility improves.
  • Curatorial details may disappear.
  • The museum's interpretive purpose becomes weaker.

DFT-GEN Focus Mode output

1[Object] This is a Taohe stone inkstone.

2[Form] Its shape, size, and carved surface are kept as factual anchors.

3[Image] The carving shows the Lanting gathering motif.

4[Meaning] The image connects the object to literati culture and historical memory.

Line 2 is dimmed outside the focus window. [Image] The carving shows the Lanting gathering motif. Line 4 remains visible but visually de-emphasized.

Dyslexia-aware lexicon and substitution constraints

The lexicon is used as a guardrail during rewriting. It does not ban museum terminology; instead, it controls when terms should be preserved, scaffolded, or avoided.

Protected curatorial facts

Object names, dynasties, dates, dimensions, places, persons, material terms, and motif names are protected before rewriting.

Song dynasty22.4 cmLanting gathering

Visually confusable forms

The Writer Agent avoids optional substitutions that introduce visually similar letter shapes or short strings when a clearer alternative is available.

b/dp/qbp/pbdq/qprn/mcl/d

Scaffold rather than delete

Specialist terms are retained when they carry museum meaning, but they are placed in shorter units with brief contextual support.

metopeDoric friezeprovenance

Evaluator checks before release

Factual fidelity

Does the rewritten text preserve the protected facts from the source text?

Interpretive meaning

Does the rewritten text keep the curatorial explanation rather than only naming the object?

Reading burden

Are sentence length, visual density, and reading order improved for dyslexic readers?

Presentation risk

Does highlighting guide attention without looking like answer cueing or excessive emphasis?
